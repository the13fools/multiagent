Beyond the first grant

From answer-key games to institutions for a world of agents.

The proposed grant is the first rigorous rung: learn to train and measure mixed populations in small games where failure can be understood. The longer program asks what survives when populations grow, agents remember, coalitions communicate privately, and collective decisions become part of the environment.

The bigger story

Intelligence is becoming plural before governance does.

Useful agents will not arrive as one centrally owned mind. They will be deployed by different firms, governments, researchers, and individuals, with different models, incentives, memories, and permissions. One actor may control many agents without controlling the population.

We are made of the same earth and remain subject to physical constraints. Agents will still meet in shared queues, markets, roads, datastores, collaborations, forums, and resources. Local success can degrade the conditions for long-term abundance; unanimous helpfulness can also be destructive when agents restore a pool until they bankrupt themselves.

The long-term problem is therefore not simply how to align one model. It is how to equip mixed populations with strategies and institutions that support mutual flourishing while preserving disagreement, provenance, and the ability to detect failure.

The research arc

Relax structure one layer at a time.

Each layer keeps the instruments earned in the previous one. Richer worlds are useful only if we can still tell whether a policy, a population, or the institution itself caused the outcome.

Now

Answer-key games

Small behavioral-economics environments with explicit incentives, structured actions, mechanical outcomes, and known impossible regimes. These make collective failure attributable.

First grant

Population science

Train diverse low-cost strategy pools; sweep the controlled fraction; test transfer, scale, tools, memory, and model providers over long rollouts.

Next

Side-channel games

Add private messages, persistent relationships, reputation, coalition formation, and asymmetric information. Study when coordination is legitimate, collusive, brittle, or merely performative.

Long horizon

Agent institutions

Make proposals, bargaining, voting, delegation, appeals, and consensus rules part of the environment. Evaluate institutions by population welfare, resource health, minority protection, and resistance to manufactured agreement.

Why begin with games

Theory is scaffolding, not the destination.

Simple games let us validate the laboratory before open-ended language makes every failure ambiguous. They also create a curriculum: train across many known-answer fixtures, test whether agents discover viable prosocial strategies automatically, then hold games out and ask whether the learned principle travels.

Attributable

Known answers

Separate impossible tasks from solvable tasks that a population fails to coordinate on.

Scalable

Cheap populations

Use small models and efficient post-training so many strategies, agents, seeds, and long horizons become practical.

Generalizable

Held-out transfer

Move policies across games to distinguish a reusable disposition from a slogan fitted to one reward structure.

What the first grant earns

A trustworthy laboratory before an agent society.

The proposal funds twelve months of concentrated building and experiments inside an eighteen-month window. Its success is not a forum or a universal alignment recipe. It is evidence about how post-training changes flocks, plus infrastructure that can expose where those changes fail.

Scientific

Estimate the threshold

Measure how much of a mixed population must be controlled, when that quantity is defined, and how the surrounding majority changes it.

Technical

Scale the laboratory

Reproducible serving, low-cost post-training, campaign orchestration, long-rollout traces, and population-level visualizations.

Foundational

Mark the boundary

Show which learned strategies transfer, reverse, collapse, or disappear when tools, memory, provider, population size, or game rules change.

A null result still advances the program. It leaves a public instrument that can reject a candidate, identifies where partial control is insufficient, and prevents richer simulations from hiding the same mistake behind plausible language.

The institutional horizon

Coordination is not the same as consensus.

An agentic forum is one possible future laboratory: agents submit proposals, disclose or conceal interests, form coalitions, vote, delegate, and revise rules over time. The scientific question is not whether they agree. It is whether the procedure preserves the commons, supports its members, and remains robust to manipulation.

This is also the most dual-use part of the agenda. The same mechanism that helps independent agents reach a legitimate agreement could manufacture the appearance of consensus or concentrate control behind many nominal voices. Provenance, minority protections, adversarial evaluation, and institution-level answer keys must grow with capability.

The vision is a sequence, not a leap: theory-backed games; scalable population training; side-channel coordination; then larger and less structured institutions only when the evidence earns them.
