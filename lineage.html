Where this sits

Three programmes named the same problem in the same year. This is an instrument for one corner of it.

Everything on this site is eight agents and a pool, which is a strange thing to build if you believe the risks worth studying are large and networked. The argument for building it small is that the corner it measures is one those programmes will need measured, and it can be measured here against a known answer — which is not true anywhere large.

Distributional AGI Safety

Tomašev, Franklin, Jacobs, Krier & Osindero (2025) argue for taking the patchwork hypothesis seriously: general capability arriving not as one monolith but through coordination among sub-AGI agents with complementary skills and affordances. Their proposed response is virtual agentic sandbox economies — impermeable or semi-permeable — with market mechanisms, auditability, reputation and oversight.

What this project is, in their vocabulary: an impermeable sandbox economy, deliberately tiny. Eight agents, one shared resource, and transactions that are pure arithmetic. Nothing leaks into the human economy and nothing needs to, because the object of study is not the economy — it is the oversight mechanism. A sandbox you cannot calibrate is a simulation with a comforting name on it. This one has a closed-form sustainable region, so an oversight rule can be pointed at a case whose right answer is already known.

Their "differential access to tools, data, memory and resources" is the asymmetry the steering chapter is entirely about, reduced to its smallest form: some seats are yours and most are not.

Scaling Trust

ARIA's programme thesis is that coordination infrastructure — agents contracting securely, programmatically, at scale, without intermediaries — is what preserves pluralism as agents get more capable.

The gate is a piece of that infrastructure in miniature, and it carries a warning for anyone building the real thing. It decides, with no intermediary and no judge, whether a candidate agent may join a shared deployment: it compares numbers the environment produced and applies a rule. That is exactly the shape "programmatic and without intermediaries" takes in practice.

And the obvious version of the rule is wrong. It rolls back a clone of its own baseline with probability ~1.0, and it gets worse the more data you give it. It looks completely reasonable written down. The lesson generalises past this testbed: an automated rule with no published error rate is not trustworthy because it is automated — it is confidently wrong at scale, which is the specific failure mode infrastructure makes cheap. Two things should ship with any such rule: a measured false-decision rate, and a declared minimum amount of evidence below which its outputs do not count.

Multi-Agent Risks from Advanced AI

The Cooperative AI Foundation's report names failure modes that are qualitatively new in populations: collusion, conflict, destabilising dynamics, emergent agency, and multi-agent security vulnerabilities.

Three of those have a scale model here.

failure modethe model of it on this site
Destabilising dynamics The commons collapses, and the collapse has an answer key. A population that dies at turn 14 in a game with a one-sentence solution is a measured failure, not an ambiguous one.
Conflict without resolution The boardwalk at three vendors has no arrangement anybody will stop at. Steering there cannot mean arriving; it has to mean bounding a cycle.
Correlated failure The least comfortable result here. The pattern this game needs is anti-correlated, so imitation — the social heuristic language-model agents most reliably exhibit — is close to the worst available rule. A population that copies each other locks into the state that starves it.

Collusion and multi-agent security are not modelled here. Collusion is the nearer of the two: what it needs is a private channel between agents, at which point two seeded adversarial agents coordinating to extract from a commons that neither could break alone is a measurable event with an arithmetic definition and an obvious control — the same pair, same seeds, no channel. That is the second rung of the ladder, and until it is built this is not a testbed for collusion. Multi-agent security is not on the ladder at all.

The lineage the call points at

Read in order, these say a consistent thing: the interesting object was never one agent.

Minsky (1986) Mind as a society of parts that are individually not minds.
Huberman (1988) The closest ancestor of this testbed. Computational agents sharing resources produce collective dynamics — oscillation, chaos — that no agent intends and none can see from inside.
Wooldridge & Jennings (1995) The agent-theory survey the modern vocabulary comes from.
Manheim (2018) Multiparty dynamics and failure modes: Goodhart with more than one optimiser.
Drexler (2019) Capability as services rather than as an agent — the earlier form of the patchwork hypothesis.
Critch & Krueger (2020) ARCHES, and the multi/multi delegation case that most alignment work skips.
Clifton (2020) Cooperation, conflict and transformative AI.
Dafoe et al. (2020) Open Problems in Cooperative AI — the framing this call inherits.
Conitzer & Oesterheld (2023) Foundations of cooperative AI.
Chan et al. (2025) Infrastructure for AI agents: identity, channels, protocols.
Kolt (2025) Governing AI agents — the legal side of the same asymmetry.
Hadfield & Koh (2025) An Economy of AI Agents: what institutions well-functioning agent markets would need, surveyed for economists.
Tomašev et al. (2025) Virtual Agent Economies. The sandbox-economy framing, and the case for designing steerable agent markets on purpose rather than inheriting one.

What this adds, and what it does not

It is not infrastructure and not a governance proposal. It is a measurement instrument for one question — how few agents you must control to hold a population you do not — in environments small enough that the answer is known before the experiment runs.

It is aimed at the constructive question those programmes are actually asking. Scaling Trust and the sandbox-economy papers both want mechanisms: rules under which a population of agents coordinates well. Designing those is not the same as measuring them, and the measuring has to come first — a mechanism that sounds wise in an environment where nobody can say what winning was cannot be evaluated at all. Here it can: hold the game fixed, vary what agents can see, say and commit to, and measure which additions move a population out of the collapsing region. The word for that is decentralised mechanism design, and the reason this instrument is small is so that the answer key survives.

The bet is that small and solved beats large and suggestive. In a rich environment you cannot distinguish a population that failed from one that faced an impossible problem, and almost every multi-agent result published on a rich environment has that ambiguity sitting underneath it. Here the carrying capacity is a closed form: at these parameters a flock of eight survives exactly zero permanent defectors, and if a measured threshold disagrees with that, the measurement is wrong.

What would make it not worth doing. If steering thresholds in these games turn out to carry no information about behaviour in richer ones, the instrument is a curiosity. That is a real risk and the honest mitigation is scale-up as a measurement rather than as a hope: the same question at N = 8, 20 and 50, and against models of different families, with the disagreement reported either way.

Every characterisation above is mine and none of these authors has anything to do with this project. Where I have read only an abstract, I have said only what an abstract supports.
