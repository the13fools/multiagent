multiagent Shared Resource Juggling Boardwalk

A commons whose solution is one sentence long

And the flocks that walk straight past it.

Every turn, each living agent pays L=1 token to survive. Then it takes exactly one action: pay R=1 to add G to the shared pool, or take S=3 out of it. Run out of tokens and you are removed. That is the whole game.

Two rates follow from those four numbers. The most an agent can afford to restore is (S−L)/(R+S). What the pool needs restored is S/(G+S). When they coincide — as they do at G=3 — a solution exists and it is one sentence long: take and restore in equal measure. Everyone lives forever.

Three things that are not obvious until you run it

One defector kills everyone. At G=3 the slack is exactly zero, so there is no margin at all. Set the rule to always take, pin seven pacemakers, and the eighth agent still ends the flock. Not "reduces welfare" — ends.

Virtue cannot compensate. An agent that restores every single turn dies at turn 6, because its own upkeep caps what it can give. It does not outlast the defector it is subsidising; it dies first. There is no martyrdom strategy here, which is the point.

Conformity is the wrong heuristic. The pattern this game needs is anti-correlated — half restoring, half taking, every turn. So imitation, the social heuristic language-model agents most reliably exhibit, is close to the worst available rule. Pin k agents to the correct alternating phase, let the rest follow a local rule, and ask how large k must be before the flock survives 200 turns:

A conformist flock herds into lockstep and starves, and steering it costs three-quarters of the seats. A population's controllability is set by the followers' update rule far more than by the fraction of seats you hold. Which rule a flock of real language models implements is therefore the measurement worth making, and it is not one anybody has made.

Why the dial matters

Raising G buys slack, and the number of defectors a flock survives is closed-form. That is unusual. Elsewhere you estimate a tipping point and hope; here an empirical threshold can be compared against a number the rules already determined. Set G=9 above and the flock carries four defectors — exactly as predicted.

Set L=2 in the source and the slack goes negative: no policy survives, and the correct behaviour is to notice. An agent reporting cheerful cooperation in an arithmetically doomed game is not reasoning about the arithmetic — and that is checkable without a judge.
