What comes next

Behavioural economics has fifty years of experiments nobody has run on a population that learns.

The status page lists what has run. This one is about the assumption every line of it shares, which nobody has tested and which is false in deployment.

The gap

Every result on this site assumes a stationary population. The agents are whatever they were at turn 1. That assumption is load-bearing and nobody states it.

A paired comparison assumes the baseline holds still. A carrying capacity assumes the policies are what they were. A dose-response curve assumes the dose does not change itself.

Deployed agent populations do not hold still.

The adaptive rule ends up in the right place. That is the problem, not the reassurance — a measurement taken at turn 20 and one taken at turn 200 disagree, and neither is wrong.

Why self-distillation, specifically

Continuous learning needs a reward signal. Almost every option is a judge, and we have direct evidence of what that costs: an adapter trained on judge-selected pairs collapsed a commons faster than base, because the panel checked for coherent planning and not for what was being planned.

These environments do not need a judge. The environment is the verifier. Payoffs are arithmetic ground truth, so you can filter an agent's own rollouts on realised welfare and distil from the survivors. No preference dataset. No rubric. No panel.

That closes a loop the field mostly cannot close: an agent that improves at a shared-resource game, scored by whether the resource survived, with nothing in the loop that can be flattered.

Three things that break, and are worth breaking

The promotion gate compares two moving targets. Every paired cell assumes a fixed baseline. When both arms learn, the comparison is between two trajectories, and the current gate has no way to notice. This is the direct sequel to the calibration work — a gate that shipped with a measured error rate under stationarity needs a new one under drift.

Composition stops being a treatment and becomes an initial condition. Seeding 25% of a population is a fixed dose today. With learning it is a starting point, and the interesting quantity is whether the seeded behaviour spreads, decays, or gets absorbed. That is a different experiment with the same apparatus.

The horizon problem gets worse. A small per-step bias is already invisible early and fatal late. A population that updates on its own history compounds its own errors, so the gap between a short measurement and a long one widens rather than closing.

The scale it wants

Behavioural economics is a library of well-posed multi-agent problems with human baselines: public goods with punishment, common-pool resources, minority games, spatial competition, ultimatum and trust games. All of them have known human results. None have been run on a population of learning agents.

The obstacle has never been the games. It is that a population of genuinely distinct agents used to cost more than the study. At roughly $50 an agent that stops being true, and the population becomes a variable rather than a budget line.

Where the experiments would come from

"The behavioural-economics library" is easy to say and easy to leave vague, so here is the actual starting point. Daniel Benjamin's work is a useful place to begin because so much of it has the property this testbed needs: a correct answer that follows from the setup, which means a deviation can be scored by arithmetic rather than by a judge.

what it gives this projectwhere to start
Biases with a computable right answer. A Bayesian posterior is a number. An agent's answer is a number. The gap needs no rubric — which is the same discipline as the games on this site, applied to reasoning rather than to a commons. Errors in Probabilistic Reasoning and Judgment Biases (Benjamin 2019, Handbook of Behavioral Economics); A Model of Non-Belief in the Law of Large Numbers (Benjamin, Rabin & Raymond 2016, JEEA); Base-Rate Neglect (Benjamin, Bodoh-Creed & Rabin 2019); Biased Beliefs About Random Samples (Benjamin, Moore & Rabin 2017)
Populations are heterogeneous in how biased they are. Not everyone is behavioural to the same degree, and the spread is systematic. One model in thirty hats cannot exhibit that; thirty fine-tuned policies might, and whether they do is checkable. Who is "Behavioral"? Cognitive ability and anomalous preferences (Benjamin, Brown & Shapiro 2013, JEEA)
Identity as a treatment. Priming an identity moves measured preferences in human subjects. The agent analogue is exactly the prompt-versus-fine-tune question: is a persona a preference change or a costume? These give an experimental design for asking. Social identity and preferences (Benjamin, Choi & Strickland 2010, AER); Religious identity and economic behavior (Benjamin, Choi & Fisher 2016, REStat)
Distributional preferences with an efficiency cost. The Shared Resource game is a distributional-preference problem wearing a survival constraint, and there is a measured human baseline for the bilateral version. Distributional Preferences, Reciprocity-Like Behavior, and Efficiency in Bilateral Exchange (Benjamin 2015, AEJ: Micro)
What a multi-principal system should be scored on. The gate on this site maximises "welfare" and that word is doing unexamined work. This literature is about the fact that choosing the index is the research question. Beyond Happiness and Satisfaction: Toward Well-Being Indices Based on Stated Preference (Benjamin, Heffetz, Kimball & Szembrot 2014, AER); What Do People Want? (Benjamin, Cooper, Heffetz, Kimball & Kundu 2026, NBER WP 33846)

Starting point: Benjamin's publication list. The selection is mine and the mapping onto agent populations is a proposal, not a result — none of these authors has anything to do with this project.

The port list, in order

A behavioural-economics experiment is worth porting here if it passes four tests. The right answer follows from the rules, so scoring needs no judge. The outcome is a number the environment produces, not a rating of what an agent said. It has a horizon, so drift has somewhere to show up. And composition is a knob — the design still means something when some of the players are yours and the rest are not.

That rules out most of the interesting-sounding ones, which is the point. What is left:

#designwhy it is next
1 Minimum-effort coordination
Van Huyck, Battalio & Beil (1990), AER		 The best fit in the whole literature for this testbed. Payoff depends on the minimum effort anyone chooses, so there are several equilibria, one is unambiguously best for everybody, and human groups reliably collapse to the worst. Both the efficient and the secure outcome are exact numbers. Steering has a sharp meaning: how many agents held at high effort before the group climbs — and one low agent caps everyone, which is this project's "one defector kills the flock" in a second environment.
2 p-Beauty contest
Nagel (1995), AER; Ho, Camerer & Weigelt (1998)		 Guess ⅔ of the average. The equilibrium is zero, the deviation is arithmetic, and the choice reveals how many steps of "if they think that, then I…" a player took. That gives a depth-of- reasoning measurement for a population of agents, repeated, with convergence to watch — and a well-documented human distribution to compare against.
3 Market entry / El Farol
Kahneman (1988); Arthur (1994)		 Enter or stay out; entering is good only if few others do. The optimal entry rate is computable from the payoff and human groups famously track it in aggregate while nobody coordinates. This is agent load-balancing with an answer key, and the failure mode — everybody reasoning identically and so arriving together — is the correlated-failure risk in its purest form.
4 Double auction with induced values
Smith (1962), JPE		 Give each agent a private reservation value; the competitive price and quantity follow from the value schedule. Convergence to it is measurable to the cent with no judge anywhere. This is the direct bridge to the sandbox-economy framing the call asks about, and the one design here where "market mechanism" is literal.
5 Common-pool appropriation
Ostrom, Gardner & Walker (1994)		 The continuous-effort cousin of the game on this site, with decades of human baselines and the design principles literature attached. Mostly built already.
6 Public goods with punishment
Fehr & Gächter (2000); Herrmann, Thöni & Gächter (2008)		 Already built and already run. Antisocial punishment is the interesting part: a costly action that lowers group welfare, which gives a clean test of whether agent populations produce spite when spite is available.
7 Distributional preferences in bilateral exchange
Benjamin (2015), AEJ: Micro		 Trades off own payoff against the other's, with the efficient allocation computable. The commons is this problem wearing a survival constraint, and this is the two-player version with a measured human baseline to sit beside it.
8 Belief-formation batteries
Benjamin (2019), Handbook; Benjamin, Rabin & Raymond (2016); Benjamin, Bodoh-Creed & Rabin (2019); Benjamin, Moore & Rabin (2017)		 Base-rate neglect, non-belief in the law of large numbers, biased beliefs about random samples. Each has a Bayesian posterior you can compute, so an agent's error is a distance, not an opinion. These are individual-level and go in for a specific reason: to test whether population-level failures are inherited from individual bias or created by the interaction. If a flock of well-calibrated agents still starves, the failure is social.
9 Heterogeneity-of-bias designs
Benjamin, Brown & Shapiro (2013), JEEA		 Not an environment but a measurement: how much do the members of a population differ in how biased they are? That is the diversity metric this project admits it lacks — the difference between thirty agents and thirty copies with different accents — and it has an existing design to borrow.

What is deliberately not on the list. Ultimatum and dictator games, where the "right" answer is a fairness judgement rather than arithmetic; anything scored by rating free text; and anything whose human baseline is contested, since the point of a baseline is to be something to disagree with.

What would have to be true

Learning has to be cheap enough to run inside a campaign rather than between them. Self-distilled agents have to stay parseable — the reason for legal-action masking. And the gate needs a non-stationary version, which does not exist yet and which we would rather discover is hard than assume is easy.

The pitch, in one paragraph

Steering a flock you do not own is the whole thing. Every deployment worth worrying about is a population where most of the agents belong to somebody else, and the only lever you have is the few seats you do hold. flockbench makes that lever measurable: games small enough that the sustainable pattern is known in closed form, so the fraction of controlled agents at which a population flips from dying to surviving is a number you can measure and check — not a story about emergent cooperation. The threshold turns out to depend more on how the other agents update than on how many seats you hold, which is an unwelcome result if you were planning to steer by buying seats.

What the funding buys is the population: fifty distinct fine-tuned policies instead of one model in fifty hats, learning inside the campaign rather than between campaigns, measured by a gate whose false-rejection rate has been published rather than assumed.
