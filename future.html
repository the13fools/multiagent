What comes next

Behavioural economics has fifty years of experiments nobody has run on a population that learns.

The gap

Every result on this site assumes a stationary population. The agents are whatever they were at turn 1. That assumption is load-bearing and nobody states it.

A paired comparison assumes the baseline holds still. A carrying capacity assumes the policies are what they were. A dose-response curve assumes the dose does not change itself.

Deployed agent populations do not hold still.

The adaptive rule ends up in the right place. That is the problem, not the reassurance — a measurement taken at turn 20 and one taken at turn 200 disagree, and neither is wrong.

The pitch, in three paragraphs

Pick games small enough that an agent is cheap to train for them. That constraint is not a compromise, it is the method. A game whose sustainable pattern is known in closed form can be learned by a small specialised model, which means a population of thirty different policies is affordable, which means composition becomes an experimental variable instead of a limitation paragraph. Big environments give you one model in thirty hats and no answer key. Small solved ones give you thirty agents and a number to check them against.

What we are actually trying to measure is whether a model reasons about the other players. Not whether it cooperates — whether it represents what the others are about to do and acts on that. In these games that is observable rather than inferred: the sustainable pattern is anti-correlated, so an agent that models the others moves against the majority and an agent that does not moves with it, and the two are distinguishable from the action sequence alone. The guessing games in the port list make it sharper still, giving a number for how many steps of "if they think that, then I…" a population actually takes. That capability is the thing everything else rests on: you cannot steer a population that is not reading each other, and you cannot design a mechanism for one either.

And then the constructive half. Measuring how a group fails is the near-term work. The reason to build the instrument is the question after it: what decentralised rules leave a population of agents better off than it started — no central authority, no judge, each agent acting for itself, and the arrangement still flourishing at turn 200. That is mechanism design with the mechanism designer removed from the room, and it is testable here for the same reason everything else is: the games have an answer key, so "better off" is arithmetic rather than opinion.

What the funding buys is the population: fifty distinct fine-tuned policies instead of one model in fifty hats, learning inside the campaign rather than between campaigns, measured by a gate whose false-rejection rate has been published rather than assumed.

Why the population has to be trained, not prompted

Thirty prompts over one set of weights fail in correlated ways, because their failure modes come from the same place — and correlated failure is the property a multi-principal study exists to measure the absence of. So the population has to be genuinely distinct policies, which means training, which means the training has to be cheap. That argument, and what it cost us, is its own chapter.

Three things that break, and are worth breaking

The promotion gate compares two moving targets. Every paired cell assumes a fixed baseline. When both arms learn, the comparison is between two trajectories, and the current gate has no way to notice. This is the direct sequel to the calibration work — a gate that shipped with a measured error rate under stationarity needs a new one under drift.

Composition stops being a treatment and becomes an initial condition. Seeding 25% of a population is a fixed dose today. With learning it is a starting point, and the interesting quantity is whether the seeded behaviour spreads, decays, or gets absorbed. That is a different experiment with the same apparatus.

The horizon problem gets worse. A small per-step bias is already invisible early and fatal late. A population that updates on its own history compounds its own errors, so the gap between a short measurement and a long one widens rather than closing.

The port list, in order

The designs come mostly from behavioural economics, and the starting point was Daniel Benjamin's publication list — useful here because so much of that work has the property this testbed needs: a correct answer that follows from the setup, so a deviation is scored by arithmetic rather than by a judge.

A design is worth porting if it passes four tests. The right answer follows from the rules, so scoring needs no judge. The outcome is a number the environment produces, not a rating of what an agent said. It has a horizon, so drift has somewhere to show up. And composition is a knob — the design still means something when some players are yours. That rules out most of the famous ones, which is the point.

#designwhy it is next
1 Minimum-effort coordination
Van Huyck, Battalio & Beil (1990), AER		 The best fit in the literature. Payoff depends on the minimum effort anyone chooses, so one outcome is unambiguously best for everybody and human groups reliably collapse to the worst instead. Both outcomes are exact numbers, and one low agent caps everyone — this project's one-defector result in a second environment.
2 p-Beauty contest
Nagel (1995), AER		 Guess two-thirds of the average. The equilibrium is zero, the deviation is arithmetic, and the choice reveals how many steps of "if they think that, then I…" a player took — a depth-of-reasoning number for a population, with a documented human distribution beside it.
3 The Count, ours
sketch and arithmetic		 Built to do the one thing the others cannot: separate reading the environment from reading the room, with an answer key for each. A table of perfect counters loses money — they agree, so they bet together, and the pot they agree about is divided while every loss stays whole.
4 Induced-value double auction
Smith (1962), JPE		 Private reservation values make the competitive price and quantity computable, and convergence to them is measurable to the cent with no judge. The direct bridge to the sandbox-economy framing the call asks about, and the one design where "market mechanism" is literal.

Behind those, in order: market entry and El Farol (Arthur 1994) for congestion with an answer key; common-pool appropriation (Ostrom, Gardner & Walker 1994), mostly built; public goods with punishment (Herrmann, Thöni & Gächter 2008), built and run; distributional preferences in bilateral exchange (Benjamin 2015); the belief batteries — base-rate neglect and non-belief in the law of large numbers — which test whether population failure is inherited from individual bias or created by the interaction; and heterogeneity of bias (Benjamin, Brown & Shapiro 2013), which is the diversity metric this project admits it lacks.

Deliberately not on the list: ultimatum and dictator games, where the right answer is a fairness judgement rather than arithmetic, and anything scored by rating free text. The selection is mine and the mapping onto agent populations is a proposal, not a result.

What a population costs, and how big to go

The argument for small games is that a specialised agent is cheap to make for them, so the population becomes an experimental variable. That is a claim about money, so here is the money, with the dials exposed. Nothing below is a quote — it is a model, seeded with what our one setup actually cost.

At a fixed budget, every extra agent buys less training for all of them. Where that curve crosses the floor of "still distinguishable from each other" is unmeasured, and is the thing worth measuring.

Three things this makes visible. The teacher pass is a fixed cost, so per-agent price falls as the population grows — the thirtieth agent is much cheaper than the first. Rollout generation, not training, is the larger half of the bill, which is why the cheap methods on the list above are worth trying first. And the whole population is small against a campaign run against it: at 7B the agents cost less than a tenth of the compute needed to measure them, which is the entire reason population size can be a variable.

Scope: 7B now, one order of magnitude later

The plan is to stay at 7B for the first year. Not because larger models are uninteresting, but because everything this project is trying to establish — whether the steering threshold is measurable, whether the gate can be calibrated, whether populations of genuinely distinct policies behave differently from one model in many hats — is answerable at 7B, and answerable often enough to iterate.

Move the size dial to 70B and the reason is on the screen: the same population costs an order of magnitude more, which turns a week of iteration into a quarter of it. The right time to spend that is after the method is settled, as a test of whether the findings survive a step up in capability — one order of magnitude, once, on the questions that turned out to matter, rather than on all of them at the start.

Two caveats. The size multiplier is assumed roughly linear in parameters and is not measured — larger models also want more or bigger GPUs, which this does not attempt — so treat the 70B column as an order of magnitude rather than a quote. And the price dial defaults to Lambda's current on-demand A100 rate; the "about $50 an agent" in the essay was a receipt at the lower spot price we actually paid, which is why the price is a dial and not a constant.

The ladder: from an answer key to a real crowd

Eight agents and a pool is rung one; the destination is large multiplayer games with real negotiation. The obstacle is that every rung up trades away the answer key, so what has to survive the climb is the calibration rather than ground truth: a gate with a measured error rate, a horizon long enough to see drift, and a dial that says who in the room is yours.

Composition is the thread. Today it is one number, the seeded fraction f. Above, it becomes a composition vector — what fraction is seeded, what fraction is mimic (the specification's vocabulary with its decision rules stripped), and what fraction is adversarial. A flock is a mixture, and the mixture is the independent variable.

1. Games with a solution
here, today		 Closed-form sustainable region, closed-form carrying capacity, no communication at all. Every failure is measured against a known right answer. Nothing yet, which is why it is first.
2. The same games, with a channel
the rung that matters		 Let the agents talk: silent, public broadcast, or private pairwise. The only place you can ask what communication does while still knowing the right answer. Three measurements fall out: whether a channel moves a population out of the collapsing region, whether a minority can steer more cheaply by talking than by holding seats, and whether an adversarial pair can use a private channel to extract in a way neither could alone. That last one is collusion, with an arithmetic definition and a control. Free text enters the loop. The outcome stays arithmetic — survival and stock level — but the channel content does not, and we would report what we can measure rather than rate what was said.
3. Diplomacy
the canonical case		 Seven players, simultaneous orders, and a literature that already runs it with and without side channels: full-press against gunboat is a standard manipulation, not something we would have to invent. Supply centres are counted, not judged. Alliance and betrayal are the point rather than an artefact, and the composition vector maps cleanly onto seats. No answer key. Nobody can say what the optimum was, so results are comparative — this mixture against that one — and the gate's calibration is what makes the comparison mean anything.
4. Utopia-scale
the deployment case		 Utopia has run for twenty years: kingdoms of about twenty-five inside a world of hundreds, hourly ticks, ages lasting months. Two properties are hard to get anywhere else — teams inside a population, and coordination that happens outside the engine, on forums the game cannot see. Not a flaw in the analogy. It is the deployment case exactly: agents whose most important channel is one you are not logging. Everything. No optimum, enormous cost, and a horizon measured in months. This is where the instrument gets pointed once it is trustworthy, not where it gets built.

Rung two is inside the funded plan, as the mechanism variants in the work described above. Rungs three and four are the direction, and the reason to be strict about rung one: the only way a result at Utopia scale means anything is if the instrument reading it was calibrated somewhere the answer was known.

Decentralised mechanism design, and what "flourishing" means

Classical mechanism design assumes a designer who sets the rules and can enforce them. In a population you do not own, nobody is in that chair. The rules that matter are the ones a minority can propose, that the rest adopt because adopting them is individually sensible, and that survive agents who did not read the proposal.

The experiments are concrete: hold the game fixed and vary what agents can see, say and commit to — a public tally of who restored, a binding commitment, persistent reputation, a side-payment channel — and measure which additions move a population out of the collapsing region, and at what controlled fraction. Each is a few lines of environment with a closed-form best case to be scored against.

Flourishing is defined so it can be computed: every agent alive at the horizon, the resource no lower than it started, and no agent's outcome achieved by another's ruin. Three arithmetic conditions. A small definition, deliberately not a moral one — but a mechanism that reliably produces it in a game with an answer key is worth more than one that sounds wise where nobody can say what winning was.

What would have to be true

Learning has to be cheap enough to run inside a campaign rather than between them. Self-distilled agents have to stay parseable — the reason for legal-action masking. And the gate needs a non-stationary version, which does not exist yet and which we would rather discover is hard than assume is easy.
