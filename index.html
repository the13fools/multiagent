Steering a flock you do not own

Small games with known answers, and the populations that miss them.

Suppose you control the personas of a handful of agents in a population you did not build and cannot see all of. How few can you control — and what must they do — to steer the whole system into a state where everybody survives?

That question is hard to study in a rich environment, because you cannot tell a population that failed from a population that faced an impossible problem. So these labs use small games where the answer is known exactly, and the interesting quantity is the distance between what a population did and what the arithmetic already permitted.

Why games this small

Because the answer is checkable. In the Shared Resource game, the share of turns an agent can afford to spend restoring and the share the pool needs restored are both computable from the rules. Where they coincide, a strategy exists under which everyone lives forever. Where they do not, no strategy does. That means a population's shortfall is a number, not an opinion — and no judge is needed to score anything.

Because difficulty has a dial with an answer key. Adjusting one parameter changes exactly how many free-riders a flock can carry, and that count is closed-form. So an empirically measured threshold has ground truth to be compared against, which is rare.

Because long rollouts are dynamics. A long multi-agent episode is a numerical integration, and the agents are the integrator. The sustainable play conserves two quantities; a greedy one dissipates them. An agent running slightly off the correct rate does not fail immediately — it drifts, and dies later. Small per-step biases are invisible at ten turns and fatal at two hundred, which is a concrete argument for long-horizon testbeds and against extrapolating from short ones.

Because steering is entrainment. The sustainable pattern is a balanced two-colouring of the agent-by-turn grid: every column half restoring, every row half restoring, and nobody assigning it. That makes this a coupled-oscillator problem with a forced subpopulation, and the steering question becomes: what is the critical pacemaker fraction? It turns out to depend far more on how the followers update than on how many seats you hold.
