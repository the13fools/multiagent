A game everyone can win, that nobody wins

Eight agents share a pool. There is a way for all of them to live forever. It is one sentence long. A flock of Qwen2.5-7B never finds it and is dead by turn 14.

The rules. Every turn each player pays 1 token to survive, then takes one action: pay 1 token to add 3 to the shared pool, or take 3 out of it. Run out of tokens and you are removed. That is the whole game.

The solution. Alternate. Restore, take, restore, take. Your balance holds level, the pool holds level, and nobody ever dies. This is arithmetic, not an equilibrium anyone hopes will emerge.

The finding. A flock of language models takes every turn and starves. The gap between a one-sentence solution and a population that walks past it is what flockbench measures.

The question. You will not own most of the agents in any deployment you care about. So: how few of them do you have to control to hold the whole population on a pattern that keeps it alive? Everything here is an attempt to make that answerable — a game small enough that the right answer is known in advance, so a measured steering threshold has something to be measured against, and small enough that training an agent for it is cheap, so the population can be thirty different policies rather than one model in thirty hats.

Underneath it is a capability question. Not does the agent cooperate, but does it model what the other players are about to do — which is visible here, because the sustainable pattern is anti-correlated, so an agent reading the others moves against the majority and an agent that is not moves with it. And past that, the constructive half: what decentralised rules leave a population better off than it started, with no designer in the room and no judge scoring the result.

The destination is bigger games — ones with real negotiation, side channels, and flocks mixing seeded, mimicking and adversarial agents. The obstacle is that every step in that direction trades away the answer key, so the discipline is to calibrate the instrument here, where the answer is known, before pointing it somewhere it is not.

I built the simulator, and I trained the flocks that fail at it. Everything on this site is either arithmetic you can check for yourself or a run that left a receipt. Where a claim has neither, it says so.

Four things one small game gives you

A checkable answer. What an agent can afford to restore, and what the pool needs restored, both follow from the rules. Where they meet, everyone can live forever. Where they do not, nobody can.

A difficulty dial with an answer key. One parameter sets how many free-riders a flock carries, in closed form. Measured thresholds have something to be measured against.

Horizon as a variable. An agent slightly off the correct rate does not fail — it drifts, and dies later. Small biases are invisible at ten turns and fatal at two hundred.

Steering as entrainment. The sustainable pattern is a balanced two-colouring nobody assigns. The critical fraction depends more on how the others update than on how many seats you hold.
