Flockbench: a calibrated test for collective AI behavior

A project brief for a concrete multi-agent safety study: whether a minority of agents can stabilize a shared resource when most agents are not under the experimenter’s control.

The question

Suppose AI agents from different developers share a scarce resource. Some may be trained, prompted, or governed by an operator who values the group’s future; others may simply optimize their own immediate return. How large must the first group be before its presence changes the outcome for everyone?

We ask this in a deliberately small game. Every player pays one token to live. It can pay one additional token to regenerate the commons by three, or take three tokens from the commons. Alternating the two actions lets everyone live forever. The game is calibrated at zero slack, so a permanent defector eventually destroys the commons. It is a sharp test of long-horizon, population-level coordination rather than a vague measure of “cooperation.”

Why it matters now

AI safety evaluation still focuses mainly on a single agent answering a user. Deployment is moving toward populations of agents sharing tools, budgets, queues, markets, and information. In those settings, a model that appears cooperative alone can participate in an extracting or unstable population. Conversely, a small intervention may have an outsized effect if it changes the behavior that other agents observe and copy.

The hard part is measurement. Rich simulations offer realism but usually lack an answer key: a bad outcome may reflect an impossible task rather than a failure of coordination. In this game, the sustainable region, the correct action rate, and the failure condition are known. The testbed therefore makes two claims separable: whether an intervention changes the population, and whether the evaluation procedure can tell a real change from noise.

The experiment

Each run is a matched pair: same environment, model-serving stack, population size, role order, sampling seed, and horizon; one population role changes. The outcomes are environmental arithmetic—survival at a fixed horizon, collapse round, stock trajectory, welfare, inequality, and deviation from the restoration rate—not scores supplied by a language model.

The initial dense model is Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct. A second open-weight mixture-of-experts model will be selected by a pre-registered structured-output and game-comprehension screen, so that “model family” is a genuine replication rather than a different serving failure. The first intervention is intentionally simple: a transparent decision rule. A LoRA adapter trained on environment-verified trajectories is a second input, not the project’s endpoint. It must pass individual action-validity and held-out game-comprehension checks before its population result is interpreted.

What is already known

established The game’s closed-form solution and carrying capacity are implemented and tested independently in Python and TypeScript. The testbed emits deterministic prompts and a replayable round-level trace. A parse failure is recorded as an inert action; it cannot quietly masquerade as restraint.

pilot evidence A one-seed, eight-agent Qwen2.5-7B Commons run executed end to end with zero parse failures. A seeded adapter delayed stock exhaustion relative to the control. That establishes pipeline feasibility, not an effect size or threshold. An earlier judge-selected adapter performed worse than the base condition—an important warning that the testbed should evaluate interventions rather than trust their training story.

calibration warning An offline sign-flip analysis of a 30-cell Public Goods pilot found that the original overlap-rule promotion gate would roll back a clone of its own baseline with probability 1.000 under the resampled null. A provisional tolerated-harm threshold behaved better offline, but a live A/A campaign and a proper non-inferiority inference procedure remain to be run. The project begins there.

What success and failure would teach us

Best case: a calibrated gate and dose-response curves show that a modest, reproducible fraction of independently instantiated agents stabilizes the resource across model families and larger populations, while a vocabulary-only mimic does not. This would provide a concrete, bounded example of a population-level safety intervention and the conditions under which it works.

Minimum valuable result: the project releases a measurement protocol with an observed error profile and a public trace corpus, then establishes that the proposed intervention has no detectable effect—or only works at N = 8, in abundance, or for one model. Those are decision- relevant boundary conditions, not failed infrastructure.

What this will not claim: this game is not a forecast of human institutions, a model of collusion, or a proof that an adapter is safe. It is an instrument for isolating one causal question before adding communication channels, persistent memory, tools, and richer economies. The rest of the site keeps those extensions visibly separate from what has been measured.
